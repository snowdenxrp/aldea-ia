# Nexo AB104.452 — External Recovery Authority and Human/Governance Bootstrap

Date: 2026-09-27
Status: RESEARCH / DESIGN ONLY
Continuity: AB104.451 → AB104.452
Implementation: NOT PERFORMED

## Research question

How can Nexo establish a new trust root when no ordinary Nexo root survives, without allowing the compromised system to authorize its own replacement or turning an informal operator action into unbounded authority?

## Evidence

RFC 6024 describes trust-anchor management as including initial provisioning, modification, deletion and recovery, and recognizes that recovery from trust-anchor private-key loss/compromise requires a pre-established recovery mechanism. citeturn0search2

RFC 9334 separates trust anchors, Attester, Verifier and Relying Party roles. A new recovery authority therefore needs an explicit trust relationship and scope; it cannot simply be inferred from successful attestation. citeturn0search2turn0search4

NIST SP 800-57 treats compromise recovery, key replacement, revocation and recovery procedures as managed lifecycle functions rather than ordinary key rotation. citeturn0search5

NIST SP 800-193 emphasizes protected recovery mechanisms and recovery paths that remain trustworthy when ordinary platform controls are compromised. citeturn0search0

## Core finding

When normal Nexo authority is gone, there must be a distinct answer to:

“Who is allowed to establish the first new trust basis?”

If the answer is “the compromised system,” recovery is circular.

If the answer is “any operator,” recovery is unbounded.

Therefore:

EXTERNAL_RECOVERY_AUTHORITY = EXPLICIT_GOVERNED_TRUST_BASIS.

## Attack H1 — compromised Nexo nominates the external authority

Compromised state says:
“Authority X is now the recovery authority.”

That statement has no authority if the compromised state cannot authorize X.

Candidate:
COMPROMISED_DOMAIN_CANNOT_SELF_APPOINT_RECOVERY_AUTHORITY.

## Attack H2 — operator identity alone

A human logs in with a valid account and claims recovery authority.

If that account was provisioned or authenticated entirely inside the compromised closure, it cannot independently establish recovery authority.

Therefore:
OPERATOR_CREDENTIAL ∈ COMPROMISED_CLOSURE => INSUFFICIENT.

## Attack H3 — emergency administrator backdoor

A hidden emergency password can restore root authority.

This converts recovery into an undocumented master key.

Candidate:
EMERGENCY_SECRET_REQUIRES_EXPLICIT_GOVERNANCE_SCOPE_AND_LIFECYCLE.

## Attack H4 — external authority without scope

External authority can create arbitrary roots, change arbitrary policies and authorize irreversible effects.

That is not recovery; it is unrestricted super-authority.

Recovery authority must have:
- explicit scope;
- purpose;
- affected domains;
- maximum effect class;
- generation;
- expiration;
- audit.

## Attack H5 — single-person recovery

One human can unilaterally create the new trust root.

This creates a single-point governance failure even if normal operation was threshold-based.

Candidate:
RECOVERY_ASSURANCE_MUST_MATCH_REQUIRED_CLAIM_ASSURANCE.

High-consequence recovery may require multi-party governance.

## Attack H6 — quorum without independence

Five emergency approvers all report to the same compromised administrator or use the same identity provider.

Counting them does not establish independent authority.

Candidate:
RECOVERY_QUORUM_REQUIRES_DEPENDENCY_AND_FAILURE_DOMAIN_ANALYSIS.

## Attack H7 — recovery authority inherits old root

External authority signs:
“new root replaces old root.”

But the new root's policy, identity, hardware and dependencies remain tied to the compromised domain.

Replacement must establish new incarnations and invalidate compromised dependencies.

## Attack H8 — recovery authority restores history as truth

External governance declares:
“the newest snapshot is the correct history.”

Governance can authorize a new trust basis; it cannot magically prove disputed historical facts.

Historical claims remain separately classified.

## Attack H9 — emergency approval has no anti-replay

Old recovery authorization is replayed after the system has already recovered.

Candidate recovery authorization requires:
- unique recovery event;
- generation;
- target;
- scope;
- validity;
- nonce/anti-replay;
- expiration;
- approval identity/incarnation.

## Attack H10 — recovery authorization survives revocation

A recovery authority is revoked, but previously issued recovery tokens remain accepted.

Candidate:
RECOVERY_AUTHORITY_REVOCATION => INVALIDATE_UNCONSUMED_RECOVERY_AUTHORIZATIONS.

## Attack H11 — external authority itself compromised

An external governance organization/service becomes compromised.

External does not mean trusted by definition.

Its own:
- identity;
- governance;
- dependencies;
- revocation;
- compromise response;
must be modeled.

## Attack H12 — recovery authority circularly validates itself

Recovery Authority A validates Root B.
Root B validates Authority A.

No independent basis exists.

Candidate:
RECOVERY_TRUST_GRAPH_MUST_HAVE_AN_INDEPENDENT_ROOT_OR_PREESTABLISHED_GOVERNANCE_BASIS.

## Bootstrap options

Candidate categories:

### B0 — pre-established offline recovery root

A root exists outside ordinary Nexo operation and is protected independently.

Advantages:
- avoids circular bootstrap.

Risks:
- compromise/loss;
- operational burden;
- root lifecycle becomes critical.

### B1 — multi-party human governance

A defined group can authorize recovery through independent identities and procedures.

Advantages:
- can survive technical-domain compromise.

Risks:
- human compromise/collusion;
- availability;
- social-engineering exposure.

### B2 — hardware/governmental/organizational external root

A separate institution or hardware-backed authority provides recovery authorization.

Advantages:
- can break technical common-mode failure.

Risks:
- new dependency;
- governance/control assumptions.

### B3 — pre-committed recovery ceremony

Nexo stores a cryptographically protected recovery policy defining who/what can initiate recovery, without giving ordinary runtime components the power to self-trigger it.

The policy itself needs protected continuity and external enforceability.

## Candidate recovery ceremony

1. Declare compromise/terminal UNKNOWN.
2. Freeze normal protected authority.
3. Establish recovery event ID.
4. Authenticate external governance participants.
5. Verify their independence for the requested claim.
6. Define target root set and scope.
7. Define affected historical claims separately.
8. Define old-root invalidation.
9. Authorize new recovery generation.
10. Generate fresh identities/incarnations.
11. Establish new trust anchors.
12. Reattest relevant platform/dependencies.
13. Reconcile external effects.
14. Commit new protected generation.
15. Explicitly release from recovery mode.

No step should infer authority from the compromised system's own approval.

## Human governance is not automatically truth

A human decision can provide authorization.

It does not automatically prove:
- historical state;
- external effect state;
- hardware integrity;
- software provenance;
- currentness of every dependency.

Therefore human governance should be modeled as an authority input with bounded scope, not a universal evidence oracle.

## Recovery assurance classes

Candidate:

RA0 — documented human instruction only.
RA1 — authenticated single operator.
RA2 — independently authenticated multi-party approval.
RA3 — multi-party approval + independent evidence.
RA4 — multi-party approval + independent evidence + protected external recovery root.

The required class depends on the consequence of the transition.

These are not security scores; they are protocol classes.

## Recovery scope rule

External recovery authority should be able to authorize:

- trust-root replacement;
- generation advancement;
- credential invalidation;
- bounded restoration;
- reconciliation.

It should not automatically gain unrestricted permission to execute ordinary external effects.

Recovery authority:
!=
normal operational authority.

## Historical claim handling

After recovery at G60:

Pre-G60 claims become classes such as:
- independently validated;
- externally corroborated;
- disputed;
- UNKNOWN;
- invalidated by compromise interval.

Do not bulk-promote historical state merely because a new root is established.
